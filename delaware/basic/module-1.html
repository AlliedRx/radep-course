Course Purpose and Who This Course Is For

A defensive driving course is not a driver's license test, and it is not a punishment. It is a structured review designed for licensed adult drivers who want to sharpen their knowledge and judgment. Experience is valuable, but it also builds habits, and not all habits are good ones. This course asks you to look at yours honestly.

Delaware regulation requires every approved defensive driving course to cover a specific list of topics. You will study traffic stops, intersections and roundabouts, impairment, work zones, vulnerable road users, school buses and emergency vehicles, motorcycles, distractions, vehicle technology, your own condition, weather, aggressive driving, following distance, safety devices, vehicle and road condition, driving environments, and head-on collisions.

Every topic connects to one goal: arriving safely, every trip, without harming anyone else.

The Benefits of Completing This Course

Delaware offers two kinds of benefits to drivers who complete an approved defensive driving course: a reduction in a portion of your auto insurance premium, and an administrative point credit on your driving record. Each one has its own rules. Many drivers misunderstand them, so read this section carefully.

The 10% Insurance Premium Discount

Delaware law, 18 Del.C. § 2503(a)(6), directs the Insurance Commissioner to require a reduction in rates for a three-year period for any person who voluntarily attends and successfully completes a motor vehicle accident prevention course approved by the Division of Motor Vehicles. The details come from Department of Insurance Regulation 607.

  • Amount: 10% per vehicle when all named operators of the vehicle have completed the course. If only some of the operators complete it, the discount is prorated according to the share of operators who completed it.
  • What it applies to: the discount applies to a portion of your premium, specifically bodily injury liability, property damage liability, and personal injury protection (PIP) coverage. It covers private passenger autos, motor homes, and motorcycles owned by an individual, by spouses, or by members of the same household.
  • Eligibility: the driver who customarily operates the vehicle must have completed a Division-approved course within the 36 months before applying. Your completion certificate is conclusive evidence of eligibility.
  • How long it lasts: the discount continues until the policy renewal following three years from your completion date. Your insurer must send you notice before the discount expires.
Important: The discount is 10% off a portion of your premium, not 10% off your entire bill. Collision and comprehensive coverage, for example, are not part of the required discount. Questions about how your insurer applies it should go to your insurance company or the Delaware Department of Insurance at 1-800-282-8611.

The 15% Refresher Discount Rule

Regulation 607 rewards drivers who keep their skills current. If you complete a Refresher Course within the 180 days before your three-year discount expires, your discount increases to 15%. If you miss that window and your discount expires, you can still restore it: completing a Refresher Course after expiration, but within two years, restores the discount at 15% from the date you complete the refresher.

The DMV's own guidance puts the timing another way: take the refresher no sooner than two years and six months after your initial course, and no later than five years after it. Both descriptions lead to the same practical advice. Mark your calendar for about two and a half years after today.

Scenario: The Household Policy

Consider a driver we'll call Dana. Dana and her husband are both named operators on the family car. Dana completes this course and sends her certificate to her insurer, expecting a 10% discount. Her husband has not taken any course.

What should happen here?

Under Regulation 607, the full 10% discount applies when all named operators complete the course. Because only one of the two operators completed it, Dana's discount is prorated by the share of operators who finished. If her husband also completes an approved course, the vehicle can qualify for the full 10%. In either case, the discount applies to the bodily injury liability, property damage liability, and PIP portions of the premium.

The Administrative 3-Point Credit

The second benefit is a 3-point credit on your Delaware driving record. This credit comes from DMV Policy Regulation 2208, section 4.2.2, not from a statute. Under that regulation, when you satisfactorily complete a course that is recognized by the DMV and approved by the Insurance Commissioner's Office, a 3-point credit is entered on your driving record.

Here is exactly how the credit is applied:

  • It works on future violations only. The credit is effective on your completion date. It does not remove or reduce points you already have, and it is not applied retroactively once an action item, such as a suspension, is already in effect.
  • It is not an automatic deduction. The DMV FAQ states that the credit is "not automatically deducted from your record." Instead, the DMV uses it "in calculating DMV penalties" if you receive future violations, in certain circumstances.
  • It does not change the totals others see. The credit "does not lower total points used for employment and insurance reviews." An employer or insurer reviewing your record will still see your full point total.
  • The course is valid for three years. According to the DMV, "The course is valid for 3 years from the completion date."
  • It has limits. Course credits do not affect whether the DMV sends the 8-point advisory letter, and they do not count toward school bus operator eligibility.

Questions about the 3-point credit or a lost certificate can be directed to the DMV Driver Improvement office at 302-744-2509.

Myth: Taking this course will erase the 3 points from the ticket I got last month.

Fact: The credit does not remove or reduce existing points. It is entered on your record on your completion date and is used by the DMV when calculating penalties for future violations, in certain circumstances. The DMV states the course is valid for 3 years from completion.

Scenario: Taking the Course After the Letter Arrives

A driver we'll call Marcus already has a suspension in effect because of points on his record. He enrolls in this course hoping the 3-point credit will end the suspension early.

What should happen here?

Regulation 2208 says the credit is effective on the completion date and is not applied retroactively once an action item is in effect. Marcus will still benefit from the safety knowledge, and the credit can help with future violations, but it will not undo the suspension that is already in place. The best time to take a defensive driving course is before problems build up, not after.

Delaware's Point System

The DMV tracks moving violations through a point system set out in DMV Policy Regulation 2208, known as the Driver Improvement Problem Driver Program. Each conviction for a moving violation adds points to your record. When your total reaches certain levels, the DMV takes action.

At 14 points or more, reinstatement requires completion of a behavior modification course within the past two years, plus a reinstatement fee.

How Points Age

Points do not count the same forever. Under Regulation 2208, points count at full value for 12 months from the date of the violation, then at half value for the next 12 months. The DMV bases its actions on convictions within that 24-month period. In addition, Delaware law generally prevents the DMV from suspending a license based on a driving record more than two years old.

Serious Speeding

Very high speeds bring action on their own, regardless of your point total. According to the DMV, driving 20–24 mph over the limit brings an advisory letter. Driving 25 or more mph over brings a one-month suspension plus one additional month for each additional 5 mph; at 25–29 mph over, the driver may take a course instead, but at 30 or more mph over the suspension is mandatory. Driving 50 or more mph over the limit, or 100 mph on a highway, brings a one-year suspension.

Other Credits

  • No-point speeding credit: a first ticket in three years for 1–14 mph over carries no points if it is paid through the Voluntary Assessment Center, as a guilty mail-in in Alderman's Court, or online. This applies to Class D license holders only, not CDL holders.
  • Seat belt credit: a 1-point credit, lasting one year, if all occupants were wearing seat belts at the time of the arrest.

Scenario: Adding Up the Points

A driver we'll call Terrell is convicted of driving 12 mph over the limit in March. In August, he is convicted of running a red light. The following January, he is convicted of driving 16 mph over the limit.

What should happen here?

Using Regulation 2208: 12 mph over is 4 points, running a red light is 3 points, and 16 mph over is 5 points, for 12 points within about ten months. All of these violations are less than 12 months old, so they count at full value. At 12 points, Terrell must complete a behavior modification/attitudinal driving course within 90 days of notice or face a mandatory two-month suspension. That same conviction also carried him past the 8-point advisory-letter level. A defensive driving course completed before these violations could have given him a credit the DMV uses when calculating penalties. The bigger lesson is that a few "small" tickets can add up quickly.

Delaware Warning: The 3-point credit from this course does not prevent the 8-point advisory letter, and it does not lower the point total that employers and insurers see. The only reliable way to protect your record is to avoid violations in the first place.

A Closer Look at 2024

The IIHS compiles state data from the federal Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS). Federal and state counts are gathered differently, so the IIHS total for 2024 (126 deaths) differs slightly from the DelDOT total (130). The IIHS breakdown for 2024 shows where the risks lie:

  • 126 deaths, or 12.0 per 100,000 population and 1.27 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled.
  • Speed-related crashes accounted for 31 deaths, or 25%.
  • An estimated 33% of drivers killed (15 drivers) had a blood alcohol concentration of 0.08 or higher.
  • 36 of 62 occupants killed were unbelted.
  • 22 motorcyclists, 28 pedestrians, and 4 bicyclists were killed.
Seat Belts Work: In a June 2024 statewide survey reported in the OHS FY2024 Annual Report, observed seat belt use in Delaware was 92.3%. Yet in 2024 (IIHS), more than half of the occupants killed were not wearing a belt. The small share of people who skip the belt make up a large share of the deaths.

You will see these categories again throughout this course, and each one is something a driver can influence.

Myth: Most serious crashes are freak accidents that no one could have prevented.

Fact: The leading categories in Delaware's recent data are speed, alcohol impairment, unbelted occupants, and vehicles leaving the roadway. Each of these involves choices that drivers make, which means each one can be reduced by better choices.

The Defensive-Driving Mindset

Knowing the law is important, but laws alone do not prevent crashes. A defensive driver goes beyond the minimum. Defensive driving means driving to prevent collisions in spite of the actions of others and in spite of conditions around you. You cannot control the driver who runs a red light, the deer that leaps from the tree line, or the sudden downpour. You can control your speed, your space, your attention, and your reactions.

Five Habits of a Defensive Driver

  1. Look ahead. Scan the road 12 to 15 seconds ahead of your vehicle, not just the bumper in front of you. Looking far ahead gives you time to spot brake lights, merging traffic, pedestrians, and hazards before they become emergencies.
  2. Keep your eyes moving. Check your mirrors every few seconds and before you slow, stop, or change lanes. Look left, right, and left again at intersections.
  3. Leave yourself space. Keep a following distance of at least 3 to 4 seconds in good conditions and more in bad weather, at night, or behind large vehicles. Space is time, and time lets you react.
  4. Make yourself seen. Signal early, use your headlights when required and whenever visibility is reduced, and avoid lingering in other drivers' blind spots.
  5. Have an escape route. Always know where you would go if the vehicle ahead stopped suddenly or a car drifted into your lane. A shoulder, an open lane, or extra space can be the difference between a close call and a collision.

Why Reaction Time Matters

A typical driver needs about 1.5 seconds to perceive a hazard and begin to react. At 60 mph, your car travels about 88 feet every second. That means you cover roughly 132 feet before your foot even touches the brake pedal. And braking distance roughly quadruples when your speed doubles. These simple facts explain why speed and following distance appear again and again in this course.

Attitude Is a Safety Device

Your attitude affects your driving as much as your skill. A driver who treats the road as a competition, who takes other drivers' mistakes personally, or who is always running late takes more risks. A defensive driver expects mistakes from others, forgives them, and leaves room for them. Being right is not the goal. Being safe is.

Scenario: The Familiar Route

A driver we'll call Priya has driven the same route to work for eight years. She knows every curve and traffic light. Lately she has been checking messages at red lights and sometimes drifts into "autopilot," arriving at work without remembering parts of the drive.

What should happen here?

Familiarity can breed complacency. Priya's experience is an asset only if she stays engaged. A defensive driver treats every trip as new: a child could run into the street, a driver could run a red light, a work zone could appear overnight. Priya should put her phone away before she starts the car, keep scanning 12 to 15 seconds ahead, and notice when her attention drifts. Delaware's hand-held device law, covered in Module 8, prohibits using a hand-held device while the vehicle is in motion, and safe habits at red lights help her stay focused when traffic moves again.
