Review: Modules 1 and 2 — The Law and Speed

Module 1 Review: The Virginia Reckless Driving Problem

Module 1 explained why Virginia treats reckless driving so seriously. In Virginia, reckless driving is a criminal offense. Under Virginia Code § 46.2-868, reckless driving is a Class 1 misdemeanor, the most serious class of misdemeanor in the state. A conviction creates a criminal record, not just a driving record.

The many ways to be charged.

Virginia law describes several ways to commit reckless driving:

  • § 46.2-852 (the general rule): driving "recklessly or at a speed or in a manner so as to endanger the life, limb, or property of any person."
  • § 46.2-853: driving a vehicle that is not under proper control, or that has inadequate or improperly adjusted brakes.
  • § 46.2-861: exceeding a reasonable speed under the circumstances and traffic conditions, regardless of the posted limit.
  • § 46.2-862: reckless driving by speed, which Module 2 covered in detail.
  • Other sections in the same article cover behaviors such as passing a stopped school bus, passing on a curve or the crest of a hill, passing two abreast, and racing.

Criminal charge versus traffic infraction.

A routine speeding ticket is a traffic infraction. Reckless driving is different. Because it is a criminal charge, it generally requires a court appearance.

Attitude versus skill.

Most people in this course are not unskilled drivers. The problem is usually attitude and choices, not ability. According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), driver behavior or error is the critical reason in the large majority of crashes. The good news: attitudes and choices can change.

Remember: Reckless driving in Virginia is a Class 1 misdemeanor, a criminal offense that generally requires going to court. It can be charged for speed, for unsafe speed in bad conditions, for loss of control or bad brakes, or for any driving that endangers people or property.

Module 2 Review: Speed — When Fast Becomes Criminal

Module 2 focused on the most common path into a reckless driving charge: speed. Under § 46.2-862, you are guilty of reckless driving by speed if you drive:

  • 20 mph or more over the applicable speed limit, or
  • more than 85 mph, regardless of the posted limit.

The 85 mph threshold took effect on July 1, 2020. Before that date, it was 80 mph.

The physics of speed.

  • At 60 mph, you travel about 88 feet every second.
  • A typical perception-reaction time is about 1.5 seconds. At 60 mph, that means you travel roughly 132 feet before your foot even reaches the brake.
  • Braking distance roughly quadruples when speed doubles.
  • Crash energy rises with the square of speed. A crash at 50 mph carries about 56% more energy than a crash at 40 mph, even though the speed is only 25% higher.

The time-saved myth.

On a 10-mile trip, driving 70 mph instead of 60 mph saves about 1 minute and 26 seconds. (At 60 mph the trip takes 10 minutes; at 70 mph it takes about 8 minutes and 34 seconds.) NHTSA reports that speeding is a factor in roughly 3 in 10 U.S. traffic fatalities. That is a very high price for about a minute and a half.

Myth: If I stay under the posted limit, I cannot be charged with reckless driving.

Fact: Under § 46.2-861, driving faster than is reasonable for the conditions is reckless driving regardless of the posted limit. Heavy rain, fog, ice, a crowded road, or a work zone can make a legal speed unsafe.

Review: Modules 3 and 4 — Aggression and Risk

Module 3 Review: Aggression and Road Rage

Module 3 moved from speed to anger. Virginia has a specific law for aggressive driving, § 46.2-868.1. A driver is guilty of aggressive driving when two things are true:

  1. The driver commits one of certain listed traffic offenses, such as following too closely, improper passing, failing to yield, evading traffic control devices, speeding, or stopping on a highway; and
  2. The driver is a hazard to another person, or intends to harass, intimidate, injure, or obstruct another person.

Aggressive driving is a Class 2 misdemeanor, punishable by up to 6 months in jail and/or a fine of up to $1,000. If the driver acted with intent to injure another person, it becomes a Class 1 misdemeanor. The court may also order the driver to complete an anger management or aggressive driving program.

Aggressive driving versus road rage.

Aggressive driving is a pattern of unsafe, pushy driving. Road rage goes further: anger becomes an attempt to confront or harm someone. The two exist on the same escalation ladder: irritation, then hostile thoughts, then gestures or honking, then aggressive maneuvers, then confrontation. The earlier you step off that ladder, the easier it is.

What the research shows.

Psychologist Jerry Deffenbacher of Colorado State University studied drivers with high levels of driving anger. Compared with low-anger drivers, high-anger drivers got angrier faster, had more hostile and aggressive thoughts, and took more risks, including speeding 10 to 20 mph over the limit, making rapid lane changes, tailgating, and entering intersections on red. In driving simulations, they had about twice as many crashes as low-anger drivers.

The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety (1997) documented at least 10,037 incidents of violent aggressive driving from 1990 to 1996, resulting in 218 deaths and 12,610 injuries.

The Monash University Accident Research Centre's Reducing Aggressive Driving (RAD) program identified common triggers: being obstructed, feeling disrespected (being cut off or tailgated), time pressure, and stress from other parts of life being carried into the car.

When someone else is aggressive toward you.

  • Do not engage: no eye contact, gestures, or horn.
  • Keep your doors locked and windows up.
  • Do not drive home if you are being followed. Drive to a police station or a busy public place.
  • Call 911 when it is safe to do so, ideally hands-free or with a passenger making the call.

Module 4 Review: Risk-Taking Behind the Wheel

Module 4 looked at the behaviors that raise crash risk and the mental shortcuts that make those behaviors feel acceptable. Risky behaviors often show up together and usually share a root, such as time pressure or anger.

Following distance.

Tailgating is one of the most common aggressive behaviors and one of the easiest to fix. In good conditions, keep at least a 3 to 4 second following distance. In rain, fog, darkness, or heavy traffic, leave more. To check, count the seconds between the car ahead passing a fixed object and you reaching it.

Thinking errors that feed risk.

BiasWhat it meansHow it shows up on the road
Optimism biasBelieving bad things are less likely to happen to you than to others"I'm an above-average driver; I can handle 90." Most drivers rate themselves above average, which is impossible.
Time discountingValuing a small immediate reward more than a large future costSaving a minute now feels more important than the risk of a crash or conviction later.
Social proofAssuming something is fine because others do it"Everyone goes 85 here."
Hostile attribution biasAssuming that another person's unclear behavior was meant to be hostile"He cut me off on purpose," which then "justifies" retaliation.

Module 4 also covered risk compensation: the tendency to take more risks when we feel safer. A driver with new safety features may follow more closely or drive faster, cancelling out the protection.

Key research point: Learning to reinterpret other drivers' behavior as a neutral act or an innocent mistake, rather than a deliberate insult, reduces aggressive responses. This is called cognitive reappraisal, and it is one of the most useful tools in the course.

License suspension.

  • Reckless driving (§ 46.2-392): the court may suspend your license for not less than 10 days nor more than 6 months.
  • Racing (§ 46.2-865): racing between two or more vehicles is reckless driving, and the license suspension is not less than 6 months nor more than 2 years.
  • Exhibition driving (burnouts, wheel spinning, unnecessary zigzagging, drag racing near a group of two or more people): license suspension of up to 6 months.

Improper driving.

Under § 46.2-869, when the recklessness is slight, the court may find the driver guilty of improper driving instead of reckless driving. Improper driving is a traffic infraction with a fine of up to $500. It is at the court's discretion, never guaranteed. This course does not give legal advice; anyone facing a charge should consult a licensed Virginia attorney.

Hidden costs.

A reckless driving conviction typically raises insurance premiums substantially for years, often by hundreds to thousands of dollars per year, and some insurers may choose not to renew a policy. A criminal record can also affect employment.

Virginia legal caution: Reckless driving is a criminal charge that generally requires a court appearance. Do not assume you can prepay it, and do not ignore a court date. Consult a Virginia attorney about your specific case.

Review: Modules 6 and 7 — Changing Attitude

Module 6 Review: Understanding Your Triggers

Module 6 turned from the law to the person behind the wheel. The central idea is the trigger-action gap: the short space between something happening (a trigger) and what you do about it (your action). Aggressive driving usually comes from what you tell yourself about the trigger.

Trigger categories.

  • Obstruction: slow drivers, blocked lanes, traffic jams, drivers who will not let you merge.
  • Disrespect: being cut off, tailgated, honked at, or gestured at.
  • Time pressure: running late, tight schedules, appointments.
  • Displaced stress: anger from work, home, money, or relationships that you carry into the car. This is called displaced aggression.

Thinking patterns that fuel anger.

  • Hostile attribution: "They did that on purpose."
  • Catastrophizing: "If I'm late, my whole day is ruined."
  • "Should" statements: "People should know how to drive. They shouldn't be on the road."
  • Personalization: "This always happens to me. They're targeting me."

Anger shows up in the body before it shows up in behavior. Common early warning signs include a tight grip on the wheel, clenched jaw, faster breathing, a hot face, raised shoulders, and leaning forward. Noticing them gives you time to choose your response.

Myth: Honking, yelling, or gesturing helps me "let it out" so I feel better.

Fact: Research suggests venting tends to increase anger rather than release it. Rumination, or replaying the incident in your mind, also keeps anger going long after the event.

Module 7 Review: Strategies That Work

Module 7 gave you a toolbox. The goal is a few strategies you know well enough to use under pressure.

Cognitive restructuring (reframing).

Cognitive restructuring means catching an angry thought and replacing it with a more accurate, less hostile one. Change the interpretation, and you change the response. Some examples:

Angry thoughtReframe
"That idiot cut me off on purpose.""They probably didn't see me. I've made that mistake too."
"This slow driver is ruining my day.""They might be lost or nervous. A few extra seconds won't ruin anything."
"I have to show him he can't do that.""It's not my job to teach anyone a lesson. My job is to get home safely."

Relaxation techniques.

  • Breathing (4-4-6): breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, breathe out slowly for 6. The long exhale helps calm the body.
  • Progressive muscle relaxation: tense and release muscle groups such as shoulders and jaw, only when safe.
  • Physical reset: loosen your grip, drop your shoulders, sit back in the seat.

Deffenbacher's research found that relaxation and cognitive-relaxation approaches reduced driving anger, and the gains were largely maintained about a year later.

The three-second delay.

When something triggers you, pause for three seconds before reacting. Those seconds create space between the trigger and the action.

Planning and transition.

  • Trip planning: leave earlier and build in a buffer to remove time pressure.
  • Pre-drive transition: before starting the car, take a minute to set aside whatever happened before.

What not to do.

Avoid venting, retaliation, and rumination. Each makes anger stronger and longer.

Your personal change plan.

A good personal change plan is specific, written, and reviewed. It names a specific behavior you want to change, the trigger that usually leads to it, and the replacement action you will use instead. A specific plan like "When I'm running late and someone in front of me is slow, I will breathe 4-4-6, remind myself that a minute won't matter, and stay three to four seconds back" gives you something to actually do. If you slip, treat it as information, not failure. Figure out what triggered it, adjust the plan, and keep going.

Putting It All Together: Integrated Scenarios

These scenarios are fictional composites. Each involves ideas from several modules. Decide what you think, then open the analysis.

Scenario: Marcus and the Morning Commute

Consider a driver we'll call Marcus. He had an argument at home this morning and left 10 minutes later than planned. On I-64, the speed limit is 70 mph. Traffic is light, and Marcus pushes his speed to 88 mph to make up time. A car merges in front of him without signaling. He closes to within one car length, flashes his high beams, and honks repeatedly.

What should happen here?

Several course concepts show up at once. The argument at home is displaced aggression (Module 6). Leaving late creates time pressure, a major RAD trigger (Module 3). At 88 mph, Marcus already meets the definition of reckless driving by speed because he is over 85 mph, even though he is only 18 over the limit (Module 2). Closing to one car length and harassing the other driver could support an aggressive driving charge under § 46.2-868.1: following too closely plus intent to harass or intimidate (Module 3). If he assumes the driver cut him off on purpose, that is hostile attribution bias (Module 4). He could face Class 1 misdemeanor penalties and 6 points (Module 5). A better path: a pre-drive transition before leaving, accepting a late arrival, the three-second delay when cut off, a reframe like "they didn't see me," and dropping back to a 3 to 4 second following distance (Module 7).

Scenario: Danielle in the Rain

Consider a driver we'll call Danielle. She is on a two-lane road with a 55 mph limit during a heavy rainstorm. She drives at 54 mph because "it's under the limit." She hydroplanes around a curve and slides into a ditch.

What should happen here?

Danielle believed she could not be reckless because she was under the posted limit. That is a myth. Under § 46.2-861, exceeding a reasonable speed for the conditions is reckless driving regardless of the posted limit (Modules 1 and 2). Losing control could also relate to § 46.2-853, driving a vehicle not under proper control. The fix is simple: slow down well below the limit in bad weather and increase following distance beyond 3 to 4 seconds.

Scenario: The Parking Lot Showdown

Consider two young drivers we'll call Tyler and Jordan. They meet at a stoplight, rev their engines, and race each other to the next light. Later, Tyler does burnouts in a shopping center parking lot while a small crowd of friends watches.

What should happen here?

Racing between two or more vehicles is reckless driving under § 46.2-865, with a license suspension of not less than 6 months nor more than 2 years (Module 5). The burnouts near a group of two or more people are exhibition driving, which carries a license suspension of up to 6 months. If racing or exhibition driving with reckless disregard for human life had caused serious bodily injury to an uninvolved person, it would be a Class 6 felony under § 46.2-865.1; if it caused a death, it would be a felony punishable by 1 to 20 years in prison. The watching crowd also reflects social proof (Module 4).

Scenario: The Points Add Up

Consider a driver we'll call Andre. He received a reckless driving conviction (6 points) in March. In September of the same year, he received another 6-point conviction. He tells a friend, "It's just points. They go away."

What should happen here?

Andre now has 12 points within 12 months, which means he must complete a Driver Improvement Clinic within 90 days (Module 5). While the points count for 2 years from the violation date, each 6-point conviction stays on his DMV record for 11 years, and each reckless driving conviction is a criminal Class 1 misdemeanor. Andre's "it's just points" thinking is a form of time discounting (Module 4): he is focused on the short term and ignoring long-term costs.

Common Mix-Ups to Avoid

Some facts are easy to confuse. Keep these straight:

  • 2 years vs. 11 years. Demerit points count for 2 years from the violation date. A 6-point conviction like reckless driving stays on your DMV record for 11 years.
  • 85 vs. 80. The current Virginia threshold for reckless driving by speed regardless of limit is over 85 mph. It was 80 mph before July 1, 2020.
  • Class 1 vs. Class 2. Reckless driving is Class 1. Aggressive driving is Class 2, unless there is intent to injure, which makes it Class 1.
  • Driver behavior and crashes. NHTSA identifies driver behavior or error as the critical reason in the large majority of crashes, and speeding as a factor in roughly 3 in 10 traffic fatalities.
