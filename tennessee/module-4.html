Listen to this lesson read aloud in U.S. English.

Speeding is one of the most common reasons drivers end up in a course like this one, and it is one of the most serious. Speed affects every part of a crash: how much time you have to see a hazard, how far you travel before you react, how far it takes to stop, and how hard you hit if you cannot stop. In this module you will learn Tennessee's speed limits, the idea of a safe speed for conditions, and the physics that make speed so dangerous. You will then review key Tennessee rules of the road: work zones, the Move Over law, school buses, railroad crossings, right of way, intersections, roundabouts, passing and lane markings, and road signs. Knowing these rules protects your license, but more importantly it protects the people who share the road with you.

Learning Objectives

  • State Tennessee's general and interstate speed limits under TCA 55-8-152, and the point values for speeding.
  • Explain why a safe speed depends on conditions, not just the posted limit.
  • Describe how speed affects reaction distance, braking distance and crash force.
  • Follow Tennessee's work zone, Move Over and school bus laws, including the divided-highway rule.
  • Apply right-of-way rules at intersections, railroad crossings and roundabouts, and read pavement markings and road signs correctly.

Speed and Crashes in Tennessee

Speed is a factor in a large share of serious crashes. According to NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System, 263 people died in speeding-related crashes in Tennessee in 2023, about 20 percent of all traffic deaths that year. In 2024, NHTSA counted 224 speeding-related deaths in Tennessee, about 19 percent of the total.

The Tennessee Department of Safety and Homeland Security's TITAN 2023 Crash Facts report adds more detail. In 2023, 6,535 crashes in Tennessee involved speeding, and speeding crashes were 5.1 times as likely to be fatal as crashes that did not involve speeding. That same report found that 81 percent of speeding crashes were not on an interstate. In other words, most speeding crashes happen on the everyday roads that run through neighborhoods, towns and rural areas, not on the highway.

Tennessee Speed Limits and Points

TCA 55-8-152 sets Tennessee's basic maximum speed limits:

  • General maximum: driving "in excess of sixty-five miles per hour (65 mph)" is unlawful unless a different limit is posted.
  • Interstates and four-lane controlled-access highways: the maximum is "seventy miles per hour (70 mph)," for both cars and trucks, where posted.
  • Left-lane minimum: on those highways, driving "less than fifty-five miles per hour (55 mph)" in the left lane is unlawful.
  • School zones: where no local limit is set, driving over 15 mph while passing a school during recess with flashers on, or within 90 minutes before or after school hours while children are arriving or leaving, makes a driver "prima facie guilty of reckless driving."

Local governments and the state often post lower limits on specific roads. The posted limit is the legal maximum for good conditions, not a target.

Speeding points

Tennessee's point system, in Tenn. Comp. R. & Regs. 1340-01-04-.03, assigns points based on how far over the limit you were:

Driving below a posted minimum is also 3 points. As you learned in Module 1, 6 or more points within a year brings an advisory letter, and 12 or more points within a year brings a notice of proposed suspension.

If you were convicted of speeding, TCA 55-8-207 allows up to five points for that conviction to be removed if you complete a department-approved defensive driving course within 90 days of the conviction. This can be used only once in a four-year period, and only the points are removed. The conviction stays on your record.

The Basic Speed Rule: Safe for Conditions

A posted limit tells you the maximum speed for ideal conditions: dry pavement, good light, light traffic and an alert driver. When conditions are worse, the safe speed is lower. This idea is often called the basic speed rule, and Tennessee's point schedule enforces it. A conviction for driving "too fast for conditions" adds 3 points to your record, even if you were at or below the posted limit. "Erratic speed" is also a 3-point violation.

Slow down below the posted limit when you face:

  • Weather: rain, snow, ice, fog or smoke.
  • Darkness: when you cannot stop within the distance your headlights show you.
  • Road conditions: curves, hills, narrow lanes, gravel, potholes or construction.
  • Traffic: heavy traffic, stop-and-go conditions or traffic merging ahead.
  • People: pedestrians, bicyclists, children near schools or parks, or crowds near events.
  • Your own condition: fatigue, illness or an unfamiliar vehicle or road.

Matching the flow, safely

Driving much slower than surrounding traffic can also cause problems, because other drivers must change lanes and pass you. If you need to drive slower than normal traffic, TCA 55-8-115 requires you to use the right lane except when passing or preparing for a left turn. Never break the speed limit to "keep up," and never let another driver push you into a speed that feels unsafe for the conditions.

Alternatives to speeding

Most speeding comes from feeling late. The National Safety Council's 4-hour course outline lists "alternatives to speeding" as a core topic, and the best alternative is planning. Leave 10 to 15 minutes earlier than you think you need. Check traffic before you leave. Accept that you will sometimes be late, and call ahead from a safe, parked spot instead of trying to make up time. Speeding saves surprisingly little time on most trips, especially in town, where traffic lights and congestion cancel out most of the gain.

Myth: Speeding is only dangerous on the interstate, where speeds are high.

Fact: TITAN's 2023 Crash Facts report found that 81 percent of Tennessee speeding crashes were not on an interstate. Local roads have intersections, driveways, pedestrians, bicyclists and parked cars, and even a few extra miles per hour leaves less time to react to them.

The Physics of Speed

Speed affects three things: how far you travel before you react, how far it takes to stop, and how much force is involved in a crash.

Reaction distance grows with speed

An alert driver needs about one and a half seconds to notice a hazard and get a foot on the brake. During that time, the car keeps moving at full speed. At 30 mph, you travel about 44 feet each second, or about 66 feet before you start braking. At 60 mph, you travel about 88 feet each second, or about 132 feet before you start braking. Double the speed, and you double the reaction distance.

Braking distance grows much faster

Braking distance does not just double when speed doubles. It roughly quadruples. That is because the energy your brakes must absorb rises with the square of your speed. A car going 60 mph has about four times the energy of the same car at 30 mph, so it needs roughly four times as much road to stop once the brakes are applied. On wet or icy pavement, braking distance grows even more.

Crash force grows with speed

That same energy is what does the damage in a crash. When a vehicle hits something, all of its energy has to go somewhere: into bending metal, breaking glass and the bodies of the people inside. Higher speed means much more energy, which is why speeding crashes are more likely to be deadly. TITAN's 2023 finding that speeding crashes were 5.1 times as likely to be fatal reflects this physics.

Speed narrows your vision

At higher speeds, your eyes must focus farther ahead, and your side vision becomes less useful. You notice less of what is happening at the edges of the road, such as a child near the curb or a car about to pull out of a driveway. Speed also gives other drivers less time to judge your approach. A driver pulling out of a side street may think there is plenty of room, not realizing how fast you are coming.

Quick Check

If you double your speed from 30 mph to 60 mph, about how much longer is your braking distance?

Show answer

About four times as long. Braking distance roughly quadruples when speed doubles, while reaction distance doubles (from about 66 feet to about 132 feet).

Scenario: Just a Little Over

Andre drives the same two-lane road through his Chattanooga neighborhood every morning. The limit is 30 mph, but he usually goes about 40 because the road is wide and usually empty. One morning a child chases a ball into the street from between two parked cars.

What should happen here?

At 30 mph, Andre would travel about 66 feet during his reaction time. At 40 mph, he travels about 88 feet before his foot even reaches the brake, and his braking distance is much longer too. Those extra feet can be the difference between stopping short and hitting the child. The posted limit on a residential street exists because of hazards like this one. Driving at or below the limit, scanning for gaps between parked cars, and covering the brake near parked cars gives Andre the time and space to stop.

Work Zones

Work zones change the road in ways drivers do not expect: narrower lanes, sudden lane shifts, uneven pavement, slow or stopped traffic, and workers only a few feet from moving vehicles. Tennessee treats work-zone violations seriously.

  • Under TCA 55-8-153, speeding in a work zone where the limit has been lowered "when employees of the department or construction workers are present" carries a fine of "not less than two hundred fifty dollars ($250)." Workers' presence is shown by "signs with flashing amber lights."
  • Work-zone speeding carries more points than regular speeding, from 2 points for 1-5 mph over up to 8 points for 36 or more over (see the table above).
  • Under the Hands Free Tennessee law, a hands-free violation in a work zone with workers present carries a $200 fine.
  • Under TCA 39-13-213, vehicular homicide in a posted construction zone where a worker is killed is a Class D felony.

How to drive through a work zone

  1. Watch for the first warning sign and slow down early. Obey the posted work-zone limit.
  2. Put your phone away and give the road your full attention.
  3. Merge early when you see a lane-closure sign, and do not race to the front of the line.
  4. Increase your following distance. Rear-end crashes are common in work zones because traffic slows or stops suddenly.
  5. Obey flaggers. Their signals have the same authority as a traffic sign.
  6. Expect equipment and workers to move in and out of the travel lane.

The Move Over Law

Tennessee's Move Over law, TCA 55-8-132, protects people working on the side of the road. It applies when you approach:

  • A stationary emergency vehicle with flashing lights, such as a police car, fire truck or ambulance;
  • "a stationary recovery vehicle, highway maintenance vehicle, solid waste vehicle, or utility service vehicle"; or
  • "a stationary vehicle that is giving a signal by use of flashing hazard lights," such as a disabled car on the shoulder.

What you must do

On a highway with at least four lanes, with two or more in your direction, you must "make a lane change" away from the stopped vehicle if it is safe to do so. If you cannot change lanes safely, or you are on a smaller road, you must "reduce the speed of the vehicle, maintaining a safe speed for road conditions." Many drivers forget the second part. Moving over is the first choice, but if you cannot move over, slowing down is required.

Penalties

FirstClass B misdemeanor; fine of $250 to $500; up to 30 days in jail
Second$1,000 fine

A Move Over violation also carries 6 points on your driving record.

Scenario: Flashing Lights Ahead

Keisha is driving on I-24 in the right lane. Ahead, a tow truck with flashing lights is stopped on the shoulder, loading a disabled car. The left lane has steady traffic.

What should happen here?

Keisha should check her mirror and blind spot, signal, and move into the left lane if there is a safe gap. If traffic in the left lane does not allow a safe lane change, she must slow down to a safe speed as she passes. Tennessee's Move Over law covers recovery vehicles like tow trucks, not just police and ambulances. Failing to move over or slow down is a 6-point violation with a first-offense fine of $250 to $500.

School Buses

Children getting on and off a school bus are some of the most vulnerable people on the road. They may cross in front of the bus without looking, and they are hard to see. Under TCA 55-8-151, a driver meeting or overtaking a stopped school bus with "lights flashing and stop sign extended" "shall stop the vehicle before reaching the school bus." This applies whether you are behind the bus or coming toward it from the other direction.

The divided-highway rule

There is one exception. If you are on a highway with separate roadways, "divided by an intervening space" that is not suitable for traffic, such as a grass median or concrete barrier, you do not have to stop for a bus on the other roadway. A painted center line or a center turn lane is not a divided highway. On a two-lane road, or a four- or five-lane road with only a painted center or turn lane, traffic in both directions must stop.

Penalties

  • Passing a stopped school bus is a Class A misdemeanor with a fine of at least $250, up to $1,000. A second or later violation carries a minimum fine of $500.
  • Striking a person while violating this law is a Class E felony, or a Class C felony if the person dies.
  • School bus camera evidence may be used.
  • Passing a stopped school, church or youth bus carries 8 points, among the highest point values in Tennessee's schedule.

Stay stopped until the stop arm is pulled back, the red lights stop flashing, and the bus begins to move. Watch for children who may still be crossing.

Quick Check

True or false: On a four-lane road with only a painted center line, drivers going the opposite direction may pass a school bus that is stopped with its red lights flashing.

Show answer

False. A painted line does not create a divided highway. Only separate roadways divided by an intervening space not suitable for traffic, such as a median, allow opposite-direction traffic to continue.

Railroad Crossings

A train cannot swerve, and it may need a mile or more to stop. In a collision between a train and a car, the car always loses. Tennessee's point schedule assigns 8 points for failure to stop at a railroad crossing, the same as passing a stopped school bus.

  • Slow down as you approach any crossing, and look and listen in both directions.
  • Stop when lights are flashing, gates are lowered or a flagger signals. Never drive around lowered gates.
  • Trains are often closer and faster than they appear. If you are unsure, wait.
  • Never stop on the tracks. Be sure there is room for your entire vehicle on the other side before you start across.
  • If there are multiple tracks, a second train may be hidden behind the first. Wait until you can see clearly in both directions.
  • If your vehicle stalls on the tracks and a train is coming, get everyone out immediately and move away from the tracks, toward the direction the train is coming from, so you are not hit by flying debris.

Right of Way, Intersections and Roundabouts

Right of way rules tell drivers who goes first. The law does not give anyone the right of way. It only says who must yield. A defensive driver never insists on going first, because being "right" does not undo a crash. Failure to yield right of way is a 4-point violation in Tennessee, and failure to obey traffic instructions is also 4 points.

Common right-of-way situations

  • Four-way stops: the first vehicle to stop goes first. If two arrive at the same time, the vehicle on the left yields to the vehicle on the right.
  • Left turns: a driver turning left yields to oncoming traffic that is going straight or turning right.
  • Yield signs: slow down and be prepared to stop. Let traffic on the main road and pedestrians go first.
  • Entering from a driveway or alley: yield to all traffic on the road and to pedestrians on the sidewalk.
  • Pedestrians in crosswalks: under TCA 55-8-134, yield to pedestrians in a crosswalk without operating signals, and do not pass a vehicle stopped at a crosswalk.
  • Emergency vehicles: when an emergency vehicle approaches with lights and siren, pull to the right and stop until it passes. Failure to yield to emergency vehicles is a 6-point violation.

Signals and lane use at intersections

On a yellow light, stop if you can do so safely. A yellow means the light is about to turn red, not that you should speed up. On a red, come to a full stop behind the line or crosswalk. Signal turns early, choose your lane before you reach the intersection, and turn into the matching lane. Improper turns and failure to signal are each 3-point violations.

Roundabouts

Roundabouts are circular intersections where traffic flows counterclockwise around a center island. They are designed to slow vehicles down and remove the most dangerous crash types, such as head-on and right-angle crashes. To use one:

  1. Slow down as you approach and watch for signs showing which lane to use for your exit.
  2. Yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk and to traffic already in the roundabout, which comes from your left.
  3. Enter when there is a safe gap, and keep moving to your right around the center island.
  4. Do not stop inside the roundabout except to avoid a crash. Do not change lanes inside it.
  5. Signal right before your exit, and watch for pedestrians as you leave.
  6. Give large trucks extra room. They may need more than one lane to get through.

If an emergency vehicle approaches while you are in a roundabout, exit first and then pull over. Do not stop inside the circle.

Scenario: The Four-Way Stop

Ruth and another driver arrive at a four-way stop at about the same time. The other driver is on Ruth's right. Ruth is running late and starts to go, and the other driver starts too.

What should happen here?

When two vehicles arrive at about the same time, the driver on the left yields to the driver on the right, so Ruth should wait. Even if Ruth thought she was first, the defensive choice is to stop and let the other driver go rather than insisting. Making eye contact, waving the other driver through, and taking a second to confirm their intentions prevents this kind of low-speed crash.

Passing, Lane Lines and Driving Left of Center

Driving left of center is one of the "Fatal Four" behaviors in the National Safety Council's outline, because it can lead to a head-on crash, the most violent kind. Tennessee's basic rule, in TCA 55-8-115, is that "Upon all roadways of sufficient width, a vehicle shall be driven upon the right half of the roadway." Pavement markings show you where that is and when passing is allowed.

MarkingWhat it means
Yellow center linesSeparate traffic moving in opposite directions. Keep to the right of them.
Broken yellow line on your sidePassing is allowed when the way ahead is clear and safe.
Solid yellow line on your sideNo passing from your side.
Double solid yellow linesNo passing from either side.
White linesSeparate lanes moving in the same direction. Cross broken white lines only with care; do not change lanes across solid white lines.
Center lane with solid and broken yellow lines on each sideA two-way left-turn lane, used only to begin or finish a left turn, never for passing or travel.

Passing safely on a two-lane road

  1. Ask whether the pass is worth it. Passing a driver who is near the limit saves very little time.
  2. Make sure the markings allow it and you can see far enough ahead to finish. Never pass on a hill or curve, at an intersection or railroad crossing, or in a no-passing zone.
  3. Check your mirrors and blind spot, signal, and accelerate briskly without exceeding the limit.
  4. Return to the right lane only when you can see the passed vehicle's headlights in your inside mirror.
  5. If an oncoming vehicle appears and you cannot finish safely, slow down and drop back.

When someone passes you, stay right and hold your speed or ease off slightly. Never speed up.

Avoiding a head-on crash

If a vehicle drifts into your lane, slow down, sound your horn and steer to the right. Do not swerve left, because the other driver may correct back into their own lane at the same moment. If you must leave the road, a ditch or a fence is far better than a head-on collision. Tennessee's point schedule assigns 4 points for improper passing, 4 points for wrong way or wrong side, and 3 points for improper lane use.

Road Signs: Shapes, Colors and Meanings

Signs are designed so you can understand them at a glance, often before you can read the words. Their shapes and colors follow national standards used across the United States. Tennessee's point schedule assigns 4 points for failure to obey traffic instructions.

ShapeMeaning
Octagon (8 sides)Stop. Used only for stop signs.
Triangle pointing downYield.
DiamondWarning of a hazard or change ahead, such as a curve or merge.
RectangleRegulatory signs, such as speed limits, and guide signs.
Pentagon (5 sides)School zone or school crossing.
CircleRailroad crossing ahead.
Pennant (sideways triangle)No-passing zone, posted on the left side of the road.
Crossbuck (X)The railroad crossing itself.

Colors

  • Red: stop, yield, do not enter, wrong way, or prohibited.
  • White: regulatory signs, such as speed limits and one-way signs.
  • Yellow: general warnings. Fluorescent yellow-green: pedestrian, bicycle and school warnings.
  • Orange: work zones and temporary traffic control.
  • Green: directions, distances and exits. Blue: motorist services. Brown: parks and recreation.

Three families of signs

Regulatory signs tell you what the law requires: stop, yield, speed limits, one way, no turn on red. They are not suggestions. Warning signs tell you what is ahead so you can slow down and prepare. A yellow advisory speed plate under a curve sign shows a safe speed for that curve in good conditions. Guide signs help you plan. Read exit and lane signs early so you can change lanes calmly instead of cutting across traffic at the last second. In work zones, obey orange signs and flaggers, and remember that Tennessee uses signs with flashing amber lights to show that workers are present.

Use every warning sign as a "What if?" prompt. A "Signal Ahead" sign means the light may change as you arrive, so ease off and cover the brake. A "Merge" sign means a driver may need your space, so adjust early. When a police officer or flagger directs traffic, follow their signals even if a sign or light says otherwise.

Reflection (for your own use — not submitted)

Key Terms

Basic speed rule
The principle that you must drive at a speed that is safe for current conditions, even if that is below the posted limit.
Reaction distance
The distance your vehicle travels between seeing a hazard and starting to brake.
Braking distance
The distance your vehicle travels from the moment the brakes are applied until it stops. It roughly quadruples when speed doubles.
Work zone
An area of road construction or maintenance. In Tennessee, workers' presence is shown by signs with flashing amber lights.
Move Over law
TCA 55-8-132, which requires drivers to change lanes away from, or slow down for, certain stationary vehicles with flashing lights.
Divided highway
A highway with separate roadways divided by an intervening space not suitable for traffic. It is the only case where opposite-direction drivers need not stop for a school bus.
Right of way
Rules about who must yield. The law never gives anyone an absolute right to go first.
Two-way left-turn lane
A center lane marked with solid and broken yellow lines on each side, used only to begin or finish left turns.
Regulatory sign
A sign that states a legal requirement, such as stop, yield or a speed limit.
Roundabout
A circular intersection where traffic moves counterclockwise and entering drivers yield to traffic already in the circle.

Module Summary

  • According to NHTSA FARS, speeding-related crashes killed 263 people in Tennessee in 2023 (20 percent of deaths) and 224 in 2024 (19 percent). TITAN found 81 percent of 2023 speeding crashes were not on an interstate.
  • TCA 55-8-152 sets a general maximum of 65 mph, 70 mph on interstates and four-lane controlled-access highways, and a 55 mph left-lane minimum on those highways.
  • Speeding points range from 1 to 8, and more in construction zones. TCA 55-8-207 allows removal of up to 5 speeding points once every four years if you finish an approved course within 90 days of conviction.
  • The safe speed depends on conditions. "Too fast for conditions" is a 3-point violation even below the limit.
  • Doubling your speed doubles your reaction distance and roughly quadruples your braking distance.
  • Work-zone speeding with workers present carries a minimum $250 fine under TCA 55-8-153.
  • Under the Move Over law, TCA 55-8-132, change lanes away from stopped emergency, recovery, maintenance, utility and hazard-flashing vehicles, or slow down if you cannot move over.
  • Stop for a school bus with flashing lights and stop arm extended unless you are on a separate roadway of a divided highway. Passing a stopped school bus is 8 points.
  • Failure to stop at a railroad crossing is 8 points. Never stop on the tracks or drive around lowered gates.
  • Right of way is about who must yield. In roundabouts, yield to traffic already in the circle and to pedestrians.
  • Yellow lines separate opposite directions and white lines separate same-direction lanes. Pass only where markings allow and you can see far enough ahead, and steer right to avoid a head-on crash. Improper passing and wrong-side driving are 4 points each.
  • Sign shapes and colors carry meaning: octagon for stop, triangle for yield, diamond for warning, pentagon for school, circle for railroad ahead; orange for work zones, green for guide signs, blue for services.
