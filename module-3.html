Most drivers have felt it: a flash of heat when someone cuts in front of you, a tight jaw when the car ahead crawls through a green light, the urge to lean on the horn or close the gap so nobody else can squeeze in. Anger behind the wheel is common. What you do with that anger is what separates a frustrating drive from a criminal charge, a crash, or a confrontation that ends in tragedy. This module looks closely at aggressive driving and road rage: how Virginia law defines aggressive driving, how ordinary frustration can climb into dangerous behavior, what research tells us about why some drivers get angrier than others, and what you should do when another driver turns their aggression on you.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the two-part test for aggressive driving under Virginia Code § 46.2-868.1 and the penalties that apply.
  • Distinguish between aggressive driving, reckless driving, and road rage.
  • Describe the escalation ladder and identify the points where you can step off it.
  • Summarize key research findings on high-anger drivers, including the work of psychologist Jerry Deffenbacher.
  • Identify common triggers for driving anger, including displaced stress, as described by Monash University's Reducing Aggressive Driving (RAD) program.
  • Apply a safe, step-by-step plan for responding when another driver is aggressive toward you.

Why Aggression Deserves Its Own Module

In Modules 1 and 2 you learned how Virginia treats reckless driving and how speed alone can turn an ordinary trip into a criminal offense. Aggression is related, but it is different in an important way. Speeding is often a habit or a misjudgment. Aggression is about emotion and intent. It is about how you react to other people on the road.

Key Idea: The goal of this module is not to make you a person who never feels anger. That is not realistic. The goal is to widen the gap between the feeling and the action, so that anger stays a feeling and does not become a decision behind the wheel.

Virginia's Aggressive Driving Law (§ 46.2-868.1)

Virginia is one of a number of states with a specific aggressive driving statute. Under Virginia Code § 46.2-868.1, aggressive driving is not just "driving angry" or "driving rudely." The law sets out a two-part test, and both parts must be met.

Part 1: A listed traffic offense

First, the driver must commit one of certain traffic offenses listed in the statute. These include offenses such as:

  • Following too closely (tailgating)
  • Improper passing
  • Failing to yield the right-of-way
  • Evading traffic control devices (for example, cutting through a parking lot to get around a red light)
  • Speeding
  • Stopping on a highway

Notice that each of these, on its own, is a common traffic violation. Plenty of drivers get tickets for following too closely or speeding without being charged with aggressive driving. Something more is needed.

Part 2: A hazard or a hostile intent

Second, while committing that offense, the driver must either:

  1. Be a hazard to another person, OR
  2. Commit the offense with the intent to harass, intimidate, injure, or obstruct another person.

This second part is what turns an ordinary violation into aggressive driving. It is important to read the word "OR" carefully. A prosecutor does not always have to prove what was in your mind. If your tailgating or improper passing created a hazard to someone else, that can be enough. And if you were tailgating specifically to scare or pressure the driver ahead, that intent alone satisfies the second part, even if no one was actually put in danger.

Penalties

Aggressive driving is a Class 2 misdemeanor in Virginia. A Class 2 misdemeanor carries up to 6 months in jail and/or a fine of up to $1,000. If the driver acted with the intent to injure another person, the offense becomes a Class 1 misdemeanor, the same level as reckless driving, punishable by up to 12 months in jail and/or a fine of up to $2,500.

The statute also allows the court to order the driver to complete an anger management or aggressive driving program. That is one reason many people taking this course are here: a judge has decided that education about anger and driving is part of the appropriate outcome.

Scenario: The Slow Truck

Consider a driver we'll call Marcus. He is running late to a job site on Route 288. A pickup truck in front of him is doing about 5 mph under the limit in the left lane. Marcus closes to within a few feet of the truck's bumper and stays there for nearly a mile, flashing his high beams. He says later, "I wanted him to know he was in the wrong lane."

What should happen here?

Marcus committed a listed offense (following too closely), and by his own words he did it to pressure and intimidate the other driver. That meets both parts of § 46.2-868.1, even if no crash happened. Following that closely also created a real hazard: if the truck had braked suddenly, Marcus had no room to stop. The truck driver may have been wrong to stay in the left lane, but another driver's mistake does not give Marcus permission to commit a crime in response. A safer choice would have been to keep a 3–4 second following distance, wait for a safe opportunity to pass on the right where legal, or simply accept arriving a minute or two later.

Aggressive Driving, Reckless Driving, and Road Rage: Knowing the Difference

These three terms are often used as if they mean the same thing. They do not, and the differences help explain how a single incident can lead to more than one charge.

Reckless driving

As you learned in Module 1, reckless driving under § 46.2-852 is driving "recklessly or at a speed or in a manner so as to endanger the life, limb, or property of any person." Reckless driving focuses on the danger created. It does not require anger or bad intent. A calm, distracted driver going 90 mph on the interstate is a reckless driver under § 46.2-862, even if he is in a good mood.

Aggressive driving

Aggressive driving under § 46.2-868.1 requires a listed traffic offense plus either a hazard to someone or intent to harass, intimidate, injure, or obstruct. It often involves a target: a specific driver the aggressive driver is reacting to.

Road rage

"Road rage" is not the name of a separate offense in the Virginia Code. It is a common term for the most extreme end of driver aggression: using the vehicle as a weapon, deliberately ramming or running another car off the road, getting out of the car to confront someone, throwing objects, making physical threats, or attacking another person. Because road rage is not its own charge, the behaviors involved are prosecuted under other laws, which can include serious criminal charges and, in some cases, felonies.

Myth: "It's only aggressive driving if I meant to hurt someone."

Fact: Intent to injure raises the charge to a Class 1 misdemeanor, but it is not required for an aggressive driving charge. Intent to harass, intimidate, or obstruct is enough, and so is simply being a hazard to another person while committing a listed offense.

The Escalation Ladder

Very few road rage incidents start with someone planning to hurt a stranger. They usually build step by step. Each step feels like a small, reasonable response to what the other person just did. Looking back, the people involved often cannot believe how far it went. One helpful way to picture this is as a ladder.

  1. Irritation. Something happens: a car cuts in, someone is slow, traffic stops. You feel annoyed. Your grip tightens. This is normal and is not a problem by itself.
  2. Hostile interpretation. You decide the other driver did it on purpose, or that they are selfish, stupid, or disrespecting you. The thought "He did that to me" adds fuel.
  3. Venting. You yell inside the car, honk long and hard, or make a rude gesture. You may feel this "lets off steam," but research suggests venting tends to increase anger rather than release it.
  4. Punishing with the vehicle. You tailgate, flash your lights, speed up to block a merge, brake-check, or swerve toward the other car. This is where many aggressive driving charges come from.
  5. Pursuit and confrontation. You follow the other driver, pull alongside to yell, or box them in. Both drivers are now distracted and emotionally charged at highway speed.
  6. Violence. Someone gets out of the car, throws something, rams a vehicle, or uses a weapon. Lives are now at serious risk, and the consequences can be permanent.

The good news about a ladder is that you can step off at any rung. The earlier you step off, the easier it is. At rung 1 or 2, a single changed thought ("Maybe he didn't see me") can end the whole thing. At rung 5, your heart is pounding, your thinking is narrowed, and stopping takes much more effort. That is why this course puts so much focus on the early steps: how you interpret what other drivers do.

Scenario: The Merge on Broad Street

A driver we'll call Denise is heading home on Broad Street in Richmond after a long shift. A car from a side street pulls out in front of her, forcing her to brake. She honks. The other driver waves a hand out the window that Denise reads as dismissive. She speeds up, follows close behind for several blocks, and when the car stops at a light, she pulls up beside it and starts yelling through her open window. The other driver yells back.

What should happen here?

Walk Denise's steps up the ladder: irritation (the brake), hostile interpretation (the wave meant disrespect), venting (honking), punishing with the vehicle (tailgating), and confrontation (yelling at the light). She was one rung away from something far worse. The wave may have been an apology; she cannot know. The safest exit was at the very first rung: brake, breathe, let the car go, and remind herself she was tired and that the other driver's mistake cost her only a few seconds. Even at the light, she could still have stepped off by keeping her window up, looking straight ahead, and letting the other car leave first.

The Psychology of Driving Anger

Why do some people stay calm in heavy traffic while others boil over? Psychologist Jerry Deffenbacher of Colorado State University spent years studying this question. His research compared "high-anger" drivers, people who report getting angry often and intensely while driving, with "low-anger" drivers. The differences were clear and consistent.

What high-anger drivers do differently

  • They get angrier, faster. The same situation, such as a slow driver or a sudden lane change, produces more anger and produces it more quickly.
  • They think more hostile and aggressive thoughts. They are more likely to assume bad intent and to think about getting back at the other driver.
  • They take more risks. High-anger drivers reported more risky behavior, including speeding 10–20 mph over the limit, making rapid lane changes, tailgating, and entering intersections on red.
  • They crash more. In driving simulations, high-anger drivers had about twice as many crashes as low-anger drivers.

Why anger makes you a worse driver

Anger is a "fight" emotion. When it rises, your body prepares for action: heart rate goes up, muscles tense, and attention narrows onto the source of the threat. That narrow focus might help in a physical fight, but driving requires the opposite. You need wide attention, to see the pedestrian stepping off the curb, the brake lights three cars ahead, and the motorcycle in your blind spot. An angry driver locked onto the car that cut them off is not watching the rest of the road.

Anger also changes judgment. It makes risky moves feel justified and makes the possible costs feel far away. A driver who would never normally pass on a double yellow line may do it to "get around this idiot." Remember from Module 2 that at 60 mph you travel about 88 feet every second, and a typical perception-reaction time is about 1.5 seconds. A distracted, angry driver may react even more slowly, at exactly the moment they are driving closer and faster.

The good news: driving anger can be reduced

Deffenbacher's work did not just describe the problem. It also tested solutions. Programs that taught drivers relaxation skills, and programs that combined relaxation with changing angry thinking patterns (often called cognitive-relaxation approaches), reduced driving anger. Importantly, the improvements were largely maintained about a year later. In other words, driving anger is not a fixed personality trait. It is a pattern, and patterns can be changed with practice. Modules 6 and 7 of this course build on exactly these kinds of skills.

Thinking Traps That Fuel Road Anger

Much of driving anger comes not from what happens but from what we tell ourselves about what happened. Psychologists have identified several thinking patterns that make anger stronger and longer-lasting.

Hostile attribution bias

This is the tendency to assume that other people's actions are deliberate and aimed at you. When someone cuts you off, the hostile thought is "He did that on purpose. He thinks he's better than me." In reality, most driving mistakes are just that: mistakes. The other driver may not have seen you in a blind spot, may be lost, may be new to the area, or may be dealing with an emergency. You almost never know the real reason.

Cognitive reappraisal: the antidote

Research shows that cognitive reappraisal, deliberately reinterpreting another driver's behavior as a mistake rather than as something malicious, reduces aggressive responses. This is not about pretending the other driver did nothing wrong. It is about choosing an explanation that does not demand revenge. Compare these:

Hostile thoughtReappraisal
"That jerk cut me off on purpose.""He probably didn't see me. I've done that before."
"She's driving slow just to make me late.""She might be looking for an address, or she's nervous on this road."
"He's tailgating me to push me around.""He's in a hurry. That's his problem. I'll let him by."
"Nobody in this town knows how to drive.""Traffic is heavy and people are stressed. I don't have to add to it."

Rumination

Rumination means replaying an incident over and over in your mind. "I can't believe he did that. Who does he think he is? I should have…" Each replay re-triggers the anger. Rumination prolongs anger, sometimes for the rest of the drive or the rest of the day.

Myth: "Laying on the horn and yelling helps me blow off steam so I don't do anything worse."

Fact: Venting tends to increase anger, not release it. It also invites the other driver to respond, which can escalate a minor incident into a dangerous confrontation. Calming techniques like slow breathing work better.

What Sets It Off: Common Triggers

Monash University Accident Research Centre in Australia developed a program called Reducing Aggressive Driving (RAD). Through its work with drivers, the program identified situations that commonly trigger driving anger. Knowing your triggers helps you see anger coming before it takes over.

Obstruction

Being slowed down or blocked: congestion, a slow driver in the left lane, a car that will not let you merge, a vehicle stopped in a travel lane. Obstruction is one of the most common triggers because it gets in the way of a goal, usually getting somewhere on time.

Feeling disrespected

Being cut off, tailgated, honked at, or gestured at. These triggers feel personal, and they activate hostile attribution bias quickly. Many drivers find that the feeling of being disrespected is harder to let go of than the actual inconvenience.

Time pressure

Running late lowers your tolerance for everything. A red light that would not bother you on a Sunday afternoon feels like an insult when you are ten minutes behind for work. Time pressure is one of the most controllable triggers: leaving earlier removes it entirely.

Displaced stress

Sometimes the anger you feel on the road did not start on the road. An argument at home, a hard day at work, money worries, or exhaustion can follow you into the car. Psychologists call this displaced aggression: anger from one situation gets redirected onto a safer or more available target. Other drivers are anonymous, and you will probably never see them again, which makes them easy targets. The driver who "made" you furious may simply have been the last straw on a bad day.

Scenario: After the Phone Call

A driver we'll call Andre gets off a tense phone call with his landlord in the parking lot at work. He pulls onto Hull Street Road still upset. Within five minutes he has honked at two drivers, passed a car on the shoulder, and shouted at a pedestrian who was slow crossing at a crosswalk. Later he says, "Everybody was driving like an idiot today."

What should happen here?

This is a classic case of displaced stress. The other drivers were probably no worse than usual; Andre's tolerance was much lower because he brought anger into the car with him. Passing on the shoulder is the kind of dangerous, illegal move that can lead to charges and crashes. A better approach would have been to recognize the trigger before starting the car: sit for a minute, take several slow breaths, and acknowledge, "I'm upset about the landlord, not about traffic." If he still felt too angry to drive calmly, waiting a few more minutes would have been the safe choice.

What RAD participants found helpful

Participants in the RAD program said they especially valued two things: practical, take-home strategies they could use in the car, and hearing about other drivers' experiences. Hearing that other people struggle with the same triggers, and learning what worked for them, helped participants see their own behavior more clearly.

The Real Cost of Road Rage

Road rage is not just an unpleasant moment. It kills and injures people. The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety studied violent aggressive driving incidents in the United States and found that from 1990 to 1996 there were at least 10,037 such incidents, resulting in 218 deaths and 12,610 injuries. Those numbers count only incidents that became violent and were reported, so they do not capture the far larger number of near-misses, frightening confrontations, and crashes where anger played a quieter role.

The costs also reach the aggressive driver. In addition to the criminal penalties for aggressive and reckless driving you have already learned, a conviction can bring DMV demerit points, higher insurance premiums for years, and possible effects on employment, especially for anyone who drives for work. Module 5 covers these consequences in detail.

When Another Driver Is Aggressive Toward You

So far this module has focused on your own anger. But you also need a plan for when someone else directs aggression at you. You may do everything right and still meet a driver who tailgates, gestures, follows you, or tries to provoke a confrontation. How you respond can determine whether the incident fades away or turns violent.

The core rule: do not engage

The aggressive driver wants a reaction. Do not give one. Avoid eye contact, do not gesture back, do not honk back, and do not speed up or brake suddenly to "send a message." Your goal is to become boring, to give the other driver nothing to react to, so that they lose interest and move on.

A step-by-step response plan

  1. Create space. If someone is tailgating you, move over when it is safe and let them pass. If someone is driving erratically ahead of you, slow down and increase your following distance.
  2. Avoid eye contact and gestures. Look straight ahead. Eye contact can be read as a challenge.
  3. Keep your doors locked and windows up. This is especially important in stop-and-go traffic and at red lights.
  4. Leave yourself an escape route. When stopped, try to leave enough room in front of your car to pull around the vehicle ahead if you need to.
  5. Do not drive home if you are being followed. You do not want an angry stranger to learn where you live. Instead, drive to a police station, fire station, or a busy, well-lit public place.
  6. Call 911 when it is safe to do so. Use hands-free calling or ask a passenger to call. Give your location, direction of travel, and a description of the other vehicle. If you are driving alone, pull over in a safe, public place before using your phone in your hand.
  7. Never get out of your car to confront the other driver. If they approach your vehicle, stay inside with doors locked. If you can safely drive away, do so.
  8. Let it go afterward. Once you are safe, resist the urge to replay the incident. Rumination keeps the anger alive and makes you more likely to react badly later in your drive.
Safety Warning: If another driver is following you, do not drive home and do not pull over on an isolated road. Drive to a police station or a busy public place and call 911 when it is safe. Never leave your vehicle to confront an aggressive driver.

Scenario: Followed Off the Interstate

A driver we'll call Priya accidentally drifts into the next lane on I-295 at night, causing another driver to brake. That driver speeds up beside her, honks, and gestures. When Priya takes her exit, she notices the same car exiting behind her and staying close through two turns. Her home is three minutes away.

What should happen here?

Priya should not drive home. She should keep her doors locked, avoid looking at or gesturing to the other driver, and head toward a police station or a busy, well-lit public place such as a gas station with people around. When it is safe, she should call 911, using hands-free calling or pulling into that public area first, and give her location and a description of the car. She should stay in her vehicle. Priya did make a mistake by drifting lanes, but the right response to an angry driver is to get to safety, not to explain or apologize face to face.

Key Terms

Aggressive driving (§ 46.2-868.1)
Committing a listed traffic offense while being a hazard to another person or intending to harass, intimidate, injure, or obstruct another person. A Class 2 misdemeanor; a Class 1 misdemeanor with intent to injure.
Road rage
The extreme end of driver aggression, involving deliberate violence, threats, or use of a vehicle as a weapon. Not a single offense in Virginia; prosecuted under other criminal laws.
Escalation ladder
The step-by-step climb from irritation to hostile thoughts, venting, punishing with the vehicle, confrontation, and violence.
Hostile attribution bias
The tendency to assume that another person's behavior is deliberate and aimed at you.
Cognitive reappraisal
Reinterpreting another driver's behavior as a likely mistake rather than malicious, which reduces aggressive responses.
Displaced aggression
Anger from one part of life, such as work or home, redirected onto other drivers.
Rumination
Replaying an upsetting incident over and over, which prolongs anger.
Venting
Expressing anger by honking, yelling, or gesturing; tends to increase anger rather than release it.
